Company Pattern Drills

“Knowing a pattern is not the same as finding it in two minutes with a clock running.”

📚 Lecture 44 of 45 Phase 6 — Interview Mastery ⏱ 8 days 🎯 40 problems 💼 Google · Amazon · Meta · Microsoft · Apple · Uber
🌍
Why does this topic matter?
By now you have met every pattern interviews use. What is missing is retrieval under pressure: looking at a problem you have never seen, with a timer and a stranger watching, and knowing within two minutes which of forty tools it needs. That is a separate skill, and it is trained the way athletes train — short, timed, mixed drills, a record of every attempt, and extra work on whatever the record says is weakest. This lecture teaches no new algorithm. It teaches you to practise.
By the end of this lecture you can
  • Triage a new problem in two minutes: size limit, input shape, question word, pattern
  • Run a 45-minute round with a plan for every stage
  • Keep a problem log and use it to choose what to practise next
  • Schedule re-solves with spaced repetition
  • Drill company-flavoured sets and write a one-page cheat sheet
🧠
The picture to keep in your head: a musician practising for a concert. A pianist does not prepare by listening to recordings. They play scales every day until their fingers find them without thinking, they rehearse the hard bars far more often than the easy ones, and before the concert they play the whole piece straight through under performance conditions. Patterns are your scales, your problem log tells you which bars are hard, and a timed round is the dress rehearsal.
↩️
Built on every lecture — especially 1, 2 and 3. Lecture 1 turned a size limit into a speed target, Lecture 2 gave you six steps for attacking an unseen problem, and Lecture 3 taught testing your own code on paper. This lecture runs those same steps against the clock, across problems from the whole course, until they are automatic.

⏱️ The Two-Minute Triage

Before any code, read the problem in this order: the size limits, then the input shape, then the question word. Together they usually name the pattern before you have finished the examples. Say what you find out loud — interviewers want to hear the reasoning.

The size ladder

A typical judge runs roughly 10⁸ simple steps per second. So the largest n tells you the slowest growth rate that can pass (Lecture 1):

Largest n Growth that passes Usually means
≤ 10–12 O(n!) Try every order (backtracking, Lecture 24)
≤ 20 O(2ⁿ · n) Subsets, bitmask DP (Lecture 35)
≤ 500 O(n³) Interval DP, Floyd–Warshall
≤ 5000 O(n²) Pairwise DP, two nested loops
≤ 2 × 10⁵ O(n log n) or O(n) Sort, heap, binary search, hashing, two pointers
≤ 10⁹ and beyond O(log n) or O(1) Binary search on the answer, fast power, a formula

🎯 Running a Timed Round

A typical round is 45 minutes including introductions, which leaves about 35 for the problem. Having a plan for those minutes stops you from coding too early — the single most common way to fail.

Minutes Stage What you say and do
0–4 Clarify Restate the problem. Ask about sizes, empty input, duplicates, negative numbers. Agree one example.
4–8 Brute force Describe the simplest correct idea and its cost.
8–15 Improve Name the waste and the pattern that removes it. Get a nod before coding.
15–30 Code Write clean code, narrating the tricky lines.
30–35 Test Walk one normal case and two edge cases by hand (Lecture 3). State the final complexity.
💡
Stuck for five minutes? Say so, then work out loud: solve a tiny example by hand and describe what you did, try the brute force and look for repeated work, or ask what the answer would be if one constraint were removed. Interviewers give hints to candidates who are visibly reasoning, not to silent ones.

📒 The Problem Log

Every attempt goes into a log — a spreadsheet is fine. The log is what turns practice into a plan: after a few weeks it tells you, in numbers, which patterns you are slow at.

Date Problem Pattern Company Minutes Outcome Re-solve on
3 Mar Car Fleet Sort + stack Google 31 🟨 Needed a hint 4 Mar, 10 Mar, 2 Apr
3 Mar Simplify Path Stack Meta 12 🟩 Clean 10 Mar, 2 Apr
4 Mar Sliding Puzzle BFS over states Google 40 🟥 Failed 5 Mar, 11 Mar, 3 Apr

Spaced repetition

You remember a solution best when you re-solve it just as you are about to forget it. The simple schedule: re-solve after 1 day, 7 days and 30 days. A clean solve moves the problem to the next gap; a failure sends it back to 1 day. Re-solving means a blank page and the timer — not re-reading.

📐 Pseudocode · Choosing Today’s Practice 
function planToday(log, today):
    due ← entries in log whose next re-solve date ≤ today
    for entry in due, oldest first:
        re-solve it from a blank page, timed
        if clean:  entry.gap ← next of (1, 7, 30, done)
        else:      entry.gap ← 1                       // back to the start
        entry.next ← today + entry.gap
    weak ← the three patterns with the highest failure rate in the log
    add 2 new problems from each weak pattern          // new work goes where it hurts

Weak-pattern targeting

Once a week, group the log by pattern and compute each pattern’s failure rate (failed or needed a hint, divided by attempts). The top three are next week’s focus: re-read their lecture, then solve new problems from that lecture’s assignment. Most people practise what they already enjoy; the log stops you doing that.

🏢 Company Sets

Every company can ask anything, but candidates consistently report different emphases. Use them to shape your final weeks, not to skip topics.

Company Commonly reported emphasis Drill day
Google Graphs, DP, matrices; open-ended follow-ups (“what if the input is streamed?”) Day 2
Amazon Arrays, trees, heaps, BFS, practical design; behavioural questions in every round Day 3
Meta Speed: often two Mediums in one round. Strings, stacks, sliding windows, trees, BFS Day 4
Microsoft & Apple Linked lists, trees, strings, clean object-oriented code Day 5

📄 The One-Page Cheat Sheet

On the last day, write one page — by hand if you can, since writing is itself practice. It is for the night before, not for the interview. What earns its place:

  • Templates you must write without thinking: binary search (first true), BFS with levels, DFS on a grid, Union-Find, Dijkstra, sliding window, backtracking.
  • The size ladder and the signals table above, shortened.
  • Your own mistakes: the bugs your log shows you make twice.
  • Edge cases: empty, one element, all equal, negative, overflow, duplicates.

⚠️ Complexity & Classic Bugs

The complexity side of this lecture is the size ladder above. These are the bugs that cost the most interviews across the whole course — check for them in the last five minutes of every round.

Bug Symptom Fix
int overflow in sums, products or (lo + hi) Negative or wrong answers on large input long; lo + (hi - lo) / 2
Off-by-one in loop bounds or binary search Misses the last element; loops forever Test n = 1 and n = 2 by hand
Marking BFS nodes visited when popped, not when pushed Same node queued many times; too slow Mark when you push
Forgetting the empty input Exception on the first test Handle it in the first line
Mutating the input the caller still needs Wrong answers in later calls Ask first, or copy
Coding before agreeing on the approach Rewrite halfway through; run out of time Get a nod on the plan first
Silence while stuck No hints, no credit for reasoning Think out loud from the first minute

💪 In-Lecture Practice Problems

Five problems from five different lectures, chosen so that triage — not a memorised solution — is what solves them.

Problem 01 · Triage: “valid” → Stack
Minimum Remove to Make Valid Parentheses
🔗 LC 1249 - Minimum Remove to Make Valid Parentheses
Medium Stack of unmatched indices Meta · Amazon
›
Problem

A string contains letters and the characters "(" and ")". Remove the fewest parentheses so that the result is valid, and return any such result.

"lee(t(c)o)de)" → "lee(t(c)o)de" "a)b(c)d" → "ab(c)d" "))((" → ""
Hints
▶Hint 1 — a nudge

Which parentheses must go? Think about a ")" that arrives when nothing is open, and a "(" that is never closed.

▶Hint 2 — the key idea

Push the index of each "(" on a stack and pop it when a ")" matches. Unmatched ")" are removed as you meet them; whatever "(" indices remain at the end are removed too.

Solution

Triage: “valid parentheses” means a stack (Lecture 15); n up to 10⁵ means one pass. The twist compared with checking validity is that you must know where the bad characters are, so the stack holds indices. Every unmatched parenthesis has to be removed — no string that keeps one can be valid — and removing only those is enough, which is why this is the minimum. Mark removals in a character array and build the result at the end, instead of deleting from a string inside the loop (which would make it O(n²)).

▶Full solution
📐 Pseudocode · Remove Exactly the Unmatched 
function minRemoveToMakeValid(s):
    keep ← array of length(s), all true
    open ← empty stack                            // indices of unmatched '('
    for i from 0 to length(s) - 1:
        if s[i] = '(': open.push(i)
        else if s[i] = ')':
            if open is not empty: open.pop()      // matched
            else: keep[i] ← false                 // nothing to close
    for i in open: keep[i] ← false                 // never closed
    return the characters s[i] with keep[i] = true, in order
TimeO(n)
SpaceO(n)
Problem 02 · Triage: expression → Stack
Basic Calculator II
🔗 LC 227 - Basic Calculator II
Medium Stack of signed terms Meta · Amazon · Microsoft
›
Problem

Evaluate a string containing non-negative integers, the operators + − * / and spaces, with no parentheses. Division truncates towards zero. Do not use a built-in evaluator.

"3+2*2" → 7 " 3/2 " → 1 " 3+5 / 2 " → 5
Hints
▶Hint 1 — a nudge

* and / must happen before + and −. Can you finish each * or / immediately and leave the additions until the end?

▶Hint 2 — the key idea

Keep a stack of terms that will be added. When a number ends, look at the operator before it: push it for +, push its negative for −, and for * or / replace the top with top * number or top / number.

Solution

Triage: an expression with precedence means a stack. The expression is a sum of terms, where each term is a product or quotient chain. So process each number as it ends, using the operator that came before it. A + or − starts a new term (pushed with its sign); a * or / continues the current term, which is the top of the stack. At the end the stack holds only terms to add. Two details: a number ends either at an operator or at the last character (check both), and division of a negative top must truncate towards zero, which Java’s / already does. The stack can be replaced by a single “last term” variable for O(1) space.

▶Full solution
📐 Pseudocode · Apply the Previous Operator 
function calculate(s):
    stack ← empty;  num ← 0;  op ← '+'
    for i from 0 to length(s) - 1:
        c ← s[i]
        if c is a digit: num ← num · 10 + value(c)
        if (c is an operator) or i = length(s) - 1:   // num has ended
            if op = '+': stack.push(num)
            if op = '-': stack.push(-num)
            if op = '*': stack.push(stack.pop() · num)
            if op = '/': stack.push(stack.pop() / num)   // truncates towards 0
            op ← c;  num ← 0
    return sum of stack
TimeO(n)
SpaceO(n), or O(1)
Problem 03 · Triage: grid + “largest” → Label Components
Making A Large Island
🔗 LC 827 - Making A Large Island
Hard Label islands, then combine Google · Meta
›
Problem

In an n × n grid of 0s (water) and 1s (land), you may change at most one 0 into a 1. Return the size of the largest island possible afterwards.

[[1,0],[0,1]] → 3 [[1,1],[1,0]] → 4 [[1,1],[1,1]] → 4 (no water to change)
Hints
▶Hint 1 — a nudge

The brute force flips each 0 and recounts the islands: O(n⁴). What work does it repeat for every flip?

▶Hint 2 — the key idea

The islands never change. Label each island once with an id and remember its size; a flipped 0 then joins the distinct islands among its four neighbours, plus itself.

Solution

Triage: a grid of islands is flood fill (Lecture 21) or Union-Find (Lecture 31); n up to 500 rules out recounting for every cell. Precompute instead. A first pass gives every island a label (starting at 2, so labels never clash with the 0s and 1s) and records each label’s size. A second pass tries every water cell: its island would be 1 plus the sizes of the islands touching it. Use a small set for the neighbours’ labels, because two neighbours often belong to the same island and must not be counted twice. If there is no water at all, the answer is the whole grid, which the starting “largest existing island” value handles.

▶Full solution
📐 Pseudocode · Label Once, Combine per Cell 
function largestIsland(grid):
    label ← n × n zeros;  size ← empty map;  next ← 2
    for each cell (r, c) with grid[r][c] = 1 and label[r][c] = 0:
        size[next] ← flood fill from (r, c), setting label to next   // cells counted
        next ← next + 1
    best ← largest value in size, or 0
    for each cell (r, c) with grid[r][c] = 0:
        touching ← set of label[x][y] for the 4 neighbours (x, y) that are land
        best ← max(best, 1 + sum of size[l] for l in touching)
    return best
TimeO(n²)
SpaceO(n²)
Problem 04 · Triage: choose k, one factor dominates → Sort + Heap
Minimum Cost to Hire K Workers
🔗 LC 857 - Minimum Cost to Hire K Workers
Hard Sort by ratio, heap of qualities Google
›
Problem

Worker i has a quality and a minimum wage. Hire exactly k workers so that each is paid in proportion to their quality and at least their minimum wage. Return the least total cost.

quality = [10,20,5], wage = [70,50,30], k = 2 → 105.00000 (hire workers 0 and 2: pay 70 and 35)
Hints
▶Hint 1 — a nudge

Everyone in a group is paid the same amount per unit of quality. Which worker in the group decides that rate?

▶Hint 2 — the key idea

The one with the highest wage/quality ratio: cost = that ratio × total quality. Sort by ratio; for each worker as the highest-ratio member, pair them with the k − 1 smallest qualities before them, kept in a max-heap.

Solution

Triage: “choose k of n” with n up to 10⁴ rules out trying groups, so look for an order to process workers in. Paying by quality means one rate per group, and that rate must satisfy everyone, so it is the group’s largest ratio. Cost = maxRatio × sumQuality. Two factors — so fix one by sorting (Lecture 29’s greedy habit) and optimise the other with a heap (Lecture 20). Walking workers in increasing ratio, the current worker has the largest ratio so far; the best group with them in charge takes the k smallest qualities seen so far. A max-heap keeps those k, dropping the largest whenever it grows past k.

▶Full solution
📐 Pseudocode · Fix the Rate, Minimise the Quality 
function mincostToHireWorkers(quality, wage, k):
    workers ← pairs (quality[i], wage[i]) sorted by wage / quality ascending
    heap ← max-heap of qualities;  sumQ ← 0;  best ← infinity
    for (q, w) in workers:
        heap.push(q);  sumQ ← sumQ + q
        if size(heap) > k: sumQ ← sumQ - heap.pop()    // drop the largest quality
        if size(heap) = k: best ← min(best, sumQ · w / q)   // this worker sets the rate
    return best
TimeO(n log n)
SpaceO(n)
Problem 05 · Triage: split into d parts → Partition DP
Minimum Difficulty of a Job Schedule
🔗 LC 1335 - Minimum Difficulty of a Job Schedule
Hard dp[days][jobs done] Amazon
›
Problem

Jobs must be done in order, at least one per day, over exactly d days. A day’s difficulty is its hardest job; the schedule’s is the sum over days. Return the minimum, or −1 if there are fewer jobs than days.

jobDifficulty = [6,5,4,3,2,1], d = 2 → 7 ([6,5,4,3,2] then [1]) jobDifficulty = [9,9,9], d = 4 → -1
Hints
▶Hint 1 — a nudge

Jobs stay in order, so a schedule is just d − 1 cut points. If you knew where the last day started, what smaller problem would be left?

▶Hint 2 — the key idea

dp[k][i] = best difficulty for the first i jobs in k days = min over j of dp[k − 1][j] + max(jobs[j..i − 1]). Walk j backwards to keep that maximum as you go.

Solution

Triage: “split a sequence into d contiguous parts” with n ≤ 300 and d ≤ 10 is partition DP (Lectures 32–35); O(d · n²) is only about 10⁶ steps. Choosing the last day’s start j leaves the smaller problem “first j jobs in k − 1 days”, and the last day costs the maximum of jobs j..i − 1. Looping j from i − 1 downwards lets that maximum grow one job at a time instead of being recomputed. Every day needs at least one job, so the first i jobs fit into k days only if i ≥ k — which is also why n < d returns −1.

▶Full solution
📐 Pseudocode · Choose Where the Last Day Starts 
function minDifficulty(jobs, d):
    n ← length(jobs)
    if n < d: return -1
    dp ← (d + 1) × (n + 1) table of infinity;  dp[0][0] ← 0
    for k from 1 to d:
        for i from k to n:                        // at least one job per day
            hardest ← 0
            for j from i - 1 down to k - 1:          // last day = jobs j .. i-1
                hardest ← max(hardest, jobs[j])
                dp[k][i] ← min(dp[k][i], dp[k - 1][j] + hardest)
    return dp[d][n]
TimeO(d · n²)
SpaceO(d · n)
